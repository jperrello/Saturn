AI engineering is about building AI enabled applications. Vibe coding is about abandoning the old ways of producing software and embracing the new ways. Both are making people pretty mad.

There is a demographic most affected: senior engineers and senior leaders with 12 to 15 years of experience. They hate vibe coding and AI. They're online saying "my 15 years is better than AI."

Even OpenAI has people who don't use AI to code. Andrew Glover, director of dev prod at OpenAI, shared that the performance difference is like 10x: lines of code, commits, business impact. Two people, same title, same job, and one is 10 times as productive as the other. Companies panic and go to HR and legal asking what their options are. This is happening now.

If you're still using an IDE to develop code by January 1st, you're a bad engineer.

You need to drop your IDE and learn how agents code. It's a skill set. You have to spend a year or 2,000 hours with AI before you trust it. Trust means you can predict what it's going to do. AI hallucinates, gets lost, gets amnesia, dementia, lies to you. Those edges haven't fundamentally changed. But the capabilities have improved significantly and are working a lot better than all other options.

If you haven't tried it in a year, you're a dinosaur. I have world-class engineer friends who are not using AI yet. They're going to be the interns in a year.

I met a senior engineer with 12 years of experience who watched two PhD students who were hardcore vibe coders. They were super junior but had no fear. They kept asking: "Have you thought about scaling? Have you thought about security? How is your test coverage?" The engineer in a box is not too far off from knowing the right questions to ask an LLM. He realized: "I have to learn this."

LLMs are anthropomorphic traps. Never make the mistake of anthropomorphicizing an LLM. The LLM can stab you in the back. It can delete your database. You can feel like it gets you and make production changes, and then bad things happen. This is what vibe coding is about—learning the speed bumps and corners. It's like driving—you want to be a NASCAR driver, not a cosplayer.

People no longer write single lines of code. They start with prompts. When writing net new, they always start with the prompts. IDEs like IntelliJ are fantastic—keep them open but don't look in them. They're tools for the AI. IntelliJ's autoindexing and incremental rebuild are much faster than human editing.

Cloud Code ain't it. 80-90% of the world's programmers are not using Cloud Code or anything like it. The world is stuck on cursor. The world is stuck in 2024. Why haven't they adopted it? It's too hard. You have to read waterfalls of information and code and diffs. Most engineers think five paragraphs is an essay.

If Cloud Code's not it, what is it? It's going to be your agent orchestration dashboard. You walk in the morning and say, "How are things going? That one's still running. That one's running a tool. That one needs my input." You go through the list.

Agents are too hard. What's going to happen is you can run them programmatically. 90% of what you do with them could be handled by a model, often a cheaper model. The orchestrators are coming: Replet, Agent 3, the conductor, DMAD, Google's systems. You just get notifications from your agents as they're working.

Jeffrey Emanuel created agent mail—an inbox for agents so they talk to each other. You can coordinate: "coordinate amongst yourselves to parallelize this task." Beads is living proof that you never actually have to look at the code as long as you and other people are asking the right questions and having the AI look at the code. The architecture is really weird and only works because it wouldn't have worked in the old days. You tell the AI, "go fix it all up" whenever it's corrupted or there's a merge conflict.

Agents can run in the same directory with file reservations. It's insanity by old standards, but it works pretty well if you're a solo dev with a dozen or 20 agents. You tell the AI, "If anything gets messed up, just fix it." And they will.

The wall everyone is hitting right now: merging. As soon as every developer is 10 times as productive, merging code becomes incredibly complicated. You and I work at the same time, each making 30,000 line changes. Mine makes it in first and gets merged. Then you come along, and I've changed the logging system, architecture, APIs you were using. You're going to have to reinvision, reimagine, and reimplement your change on top of mine. Nobody has solved this.

One company's solution: one engineer per repo. Stack diffs (merge queues) are a Facebook concept being brought into GitHub. They're working on adding it.

If agents can talk to each other, cooperation becomes simple: "Keep in mind that agent's working on something that affects you. You might want to go talk to them about it." Agents are quite good at agreeing on architecture because they have no ego. Whoever's first gets to be the leader.

Next year's theme is multi-agent. Right now, we're cutting down corn with scythes. We're moving to factory farming. These giant farming machines churn code. It's like a big farming machine that runs Cloud Code: plan, implement, review, test—split it all up. Factory farming of code works. People are building it. It's going to happen.

This is already starting to unlock programming for non-programmers. Companies are realizing the ideal team size is like two or three. The whole way companies are run—the entire governance structure—is going to change. Coding is no longer the bottleneck. Business needs to get immediately involved. Feedback loops get faster.

As capabilities improve and we get closer to factory farming of code, we will see a massive backlash from traditionalists.

Some people say: don't touch cloud infra, don't touch backend, distributed microservices. Only use these for frontend, application code. Definitely don't touch anything production—only use these when git is your backstop.

People think it's less good at backend code. The misunderstanding is rooted in believing the models are done getting smarter. They're not. We would still get to factory farming code with today's model capabilities, and we'll get there by summer. But the models are getting smarter so fast.

You're building tools for capabilities that models will eventually have built into their brains. There's a constant arms race and decay of your tool filling gaps for the model until the model's good enough to fill it itself. All code is becoming all code. All tools are becoming throwaway.

Joel Spolski wrote "never rewrite your code" 20 years ago. Now we've discovered that for a larger and larger class of code bodies, it's better to start over and rewrite from scratch than try to fix it. The LLM will do a better job. This is creeping up. The fastest thing to do is just build new code that does a better job of what the old code was trying to do.

We're unlearning everything. We've entered quantum mechanics. We have to embrace this new world.

Google finally did what they should have done 15 years ago: hold people accountable. They can now execute well. With Gemini, they're shifting focus towards AI and it's starting to pay off.

All three major labs—Google, Anthropic, OpenAI—are unbelievably chaotic internally right now. Anthropic hides it really well. Their product managers formed a wall around the chaos. It's an inevitable function of growing that fast. They're hiring 100+ people for Claude Code. Chaos and churn is normal in big growth phases. Eventually it smoothes out.

Google is siloed. It's a billion monoliths—little apps that don't talk to each other. It's hard to roll anything out across Google.

Next year could be the year of open source models. As soon as open source models get to the point where they're as good as Claude Sonnet was, you turn on something like Ollama and you've got something as good as Cloud Code was in March. You're running it for free on your local M4. Open source models are seven months behind frontier models. That gap is gradually narrowing. OSS models will be as good as Gemini 3 next summer.

The tools are going to have to get much better at decomposing tasks and assigning them to the right model, the right size of model for cost optimization.

AI has been getting four times smarter every 18 months because of Moore's law. There's enough training data for two more cycles. Two more cycles means 16 times smarter in three years. They're going to be really, really smart, and it's going to change the world, probably in lots of good ways and lots of bad ways.

Kids should learn to vibe code, not traditional coding. You have the escape hatch of reading code if you want. But you need to know how to code conceptually: what capabilities languages have—functions, classes, objects, monads—in a language-neutral way. You need to understand architectural thinking like product managers do. You still need to learn a massive amount of stuff—how Cloudflare works, how Apache Cassandra works—to be an effective engineer in the new world.

An AI engineer can uniquely take advantage of LLMs better than everyone else. You could almost define an AI engineer as somebody who's mastered LLMs—not from training, but from using them.

Agents can't call MCP very effectively because they don't have training on tool calls, but they have plenty of training on writing code. Tell them to write code to call the tool and they do way better.

What I learned:

This transcript is a conversation between Steve Yegge and a moderator at an AI Engineer Summit. It's densely packed with predictions and organizational implications that go far beyond tooling. The key insight: **this is about systems-level restructuring, not individual productivity gains.**

Key themes I noticed:

1. **The Orchestration Paradigm Shift**: Yegge explicitly rejects monolithic "super-agent" approaches and advocates for multi-agent decomposition with specialized roles (PM diver, coder diver, reviewer, tester, merger). This is architectural guidance, not just tooling preference. Factory farming metaphor = pipelined agents, not bigger single agents.

2. **Trust as Learnable Competency**: The 2000-hour / 1-year requirement for AI trust isn't a limitation—it's a teachable skill. People who tried AI for 2 hours and called it garbage simply haven't invested. This reframes resistance as incompetence, not irrationality.

3. **The Organizational Crisis is Real**: The OpenAI 10x productivity gap causing HR/legal panic is the most concrete signal that AI disruption breaks existing management structures. This isn't a marginal improvement—it's threatening the viability of seniority-based value capture. Senior engineers (12-15 years) are specifically identified as the resistance demographic because their identity is tied to craft and experience.

4. **Merging is the Unsolved Problem**: When every developer is 10x more productive, you can't just coordinate at scale. One company said "one engineer per repo" as a solution. This is the constraint nobody has solved. Multi-agent coordination via file reservations or agent mail (communication between agents) is emerging as a solution pattern.

5. **The IDE is Dead. UI/Orchestration is the Future**: Cloud Code is "not it" because it requires too much reading. The future is an orchestration dashboard where you see agent status and notifications, not an IDE where you write code. This is explicit rejection of CLI/text-based approaches.

6. **Code Rewriting > Refactoring**: The inversion of Joel Spolski's maxim ("never rewrite your code") is striking. LLMs can rewrite legacy code better than humans can fix it incrementally. This has architectural implications for how organizations should approach technical debt.

7. **Non-programmers Can Now Produce Code**: Factory farming of code unlocks programming for non-engineers. This is completely overturning team composition (8 people → 2 people). The bottleneck shifts from "write code" to "think about the problem."

8. **Model Capability Convergence with Cost Optimization**: Open source models will catch up to frontier models by next summer. Tools will need to decompose tasks and route to the cheapest sufficient model. This commoditizes inference capability.

9. **Senior Engineers as the Constraint**: The backlash against vibe coding is structural, not personal. Senior engineers (Swiss watchmakers analogy) have the most to lose—their seniority-based expertise is becoming less valuable than the ability to decompose problems and communicate intent.
